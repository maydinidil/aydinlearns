# Methodology review of the v1 design, 2026-10-02

Three independent expert reviews of `docs/superpowers/specs/2026-10-01-aydinlearns-v1-design.md`, each built on three research sweeps, with every concern re-checked by a skeptic. This file is the record behind the changes listed in design §23. Each concern shows its final (verified) severity and the refined recommendation; the spec says what was adopted and where the owner decided.

Evidence strength matters: much SQL-specific advice rests on experience reports and expert judgement, and the review says so where it applies.

## Senior SQL teacher (T-)

**Verdict.** The engine is sound, and in places better than commercial SQL courses. The sequence follows the measured order of difficulty. Grading on result sets, with a hidden edge-case dataset and planted wrong queries, gives the kind of diagnostic feedback most products lack. Spacing, interleaving, faded worked examples and a multi-day mastery rule are the right mechanisms. The weak spots are in what the learner is asked to do, and in a few rules that would quietly distort the content. Every graded item is a blank-page write. Fading reuses a single example. Grain, keys and the order in which SQL evaluates a query are never taught as ideas in their own right. DuckDB-only habits are accepted without comment. Two defects must be fixed before content generation: the prerequisite content check is built on 01's sparse graph and would remove ORDER BY, CASE and ROUND from most later items, and JOIN-03 needs CTEs that only arrive a level later. None of this blocks the implementation plan. Almost every fix is a content rule, an item kind that reuses the existing grader or multiple-choice engine, or a one-line rule change, and all of them belong in slice 0, before about 160 items are generated.

**Solid, keep:**
- The concept order matches the evidence: one table, then aggregation, then joins, then subqueries and CTEs, then windows, then business patterns. Aggregation before joins and NULL inside level 1 are right (Ahadi et al. 2015 ITiCSE; Taipalus & Perala 2019 SIGCSE; Poulsen et al. 2020).
- Grading on bags of rows, with a hidden edge-case dataset, planted wrong queries mapped to error IDs, and a content check that every planted wrong query gets caught. This is the XData approach, and it guards against the deceptive passes found in large online judges (Chandra et al. 2015; Wang et al. 2024 TOCE). Keep it at the centre of the product.
- Feedback names the misconception and shows a row diff with missing and extra rows and the first differing cell. The 60-second grace for syntax errors puts the weight on logical errors, which persist, rather than syntax slips, which learners fix themselves (Taipalus 2020 FIE).
- Lessons are instruction-first: at most 5 minutes of reading, a backward-faded worked example with adaptive stage moves, and a pretest that skips the lesson. That is right for new syntax, which has many interacting parts for a beginner (Ashman, Kalyuga & Sweller 2020; Barbieri et al. 2023; Kalyuga 2007). Do not reorder lessons to put problems first.
- One FSRS card per concept, a different item at each review, and a 30-day no-repeat rule (RULE-02). Interleaving starts after a blocked first exposure and uses a registry of look-alike concepts (Brunmair & Richter 2019; Rohrer et al. 2020).
- The mastery core: 3 unassisted first-attempt solves, on 3 different items, across 2 or more days, at least 1 interleaved. This is already stronger than N-correct-in-a-row. The changes I propose below tighten it; they do not replace it.
- Hints are available on demand through a sequential 3-step ladder, and their cost shows in the rating. Partial credit is shown but kept out of scheduling (Razzaq & Heffernan 2010; Aleven et al. 2003; 02 §3).
- Clean data first, then the raw tables with their quirks as level 4 incident tickets. Novices are protected early, and messy-data realism still arrives before applications.
- A recurring cast of managers at fictional Benelux companies with planted findings, and case checkpoints for scope (CP1: grain, denominator, which table), an intermediate count (CP2), interpretation (CP5) and a written insight against a rubric (CP6). This mirrors what NL case rounds judge (01 §1.2).
- A rules badge on every exercise (order, names, rounding), plus require_rounding so a missing ROUND fails. Expectations are explicit, which removes a top cause of opaque 'wrong answer' frustration (08 UX-03, PF-02).
- Run is free and only Submit is graded, as in real screens. No streaks.

### T-01: The prerequisite content check would remove ORDER BY, IN, CASE and ROUND from most later items

Final severity: **must-change** (reviewer: must-change; skeptic: uphold). Challenges an owner decision: no.

- **Problem:** Read literally, a GROUP BY item may not sort its output, a join item may not use CASE or IN, and a level 6 'top 3 per category, rounded to 2 decimals, sorted by category' item breaks the rule three times. The generators will either fail the check in bulk or write artificial items that never combine skills. That kills the spiral in which earlier concepts keep coming back in later work. It also makes 01's own patterns unwritable, for example AGG-02 E3 'sorted by channel and month', JOIN-01 E2 and PAT-01. The review called these prerequisites 'valid'. That is true for the curriculum map, but the graph was never meant to be an allow-list.
- **Evidence:** I traced spec §11 against 01 §3.1 (expert judgement, checked against the graph). Spiral reuse of earlier concepts is also what makes implicit review work: Choffin et al. 2019 EDM, DAS3H (modelling every skill an item touches); Skycak 2023, fractional implicit repetition (practitioner).
- **Recommendation (after verification):** Slice 0, ERRATA owner-decision entry. An item may use its target concept, any concept earlier in the amended curriculum order (01 §3.1 plus ERRATA), and a short allow-list of helpers that the prompt names explicitly (for example 'round to 2 decimals with ROUND(x, 2)'). Slice 0 also defines the construct-to-concept map the check needs. ORDER BY inside OVER maps to WIN-01, not SORT-01. Keep the prerequisite graph for the map's 'prerequisites not yet mastered' display only.

### T-02: JOIN-03's fan-out fix needs CTEs, which arrive a level later

Final severity: **must-change** (reviewer: must-change; skeptic: uphold). Challenges an owner decision: yes.

- **Problem:** Under either version of the content check, a correct JOIN-03 key cannot be written with the tools taught so far. The generator will either fail, or fall back on the anti-pattern JOIN-03 itself warns against, SUM(DISTINCT ...) (01 line 412), or on 'sum a child-level column' workarounds that do not generalise. The learner then meets the most important analyst skill in level 3, controlling grain, without the construct that expresses it. CTEs are also the easier of the two forms for novices: they read top to bottom like a plan, while a derived table has to be read inside out and its brackets managed.
- **Evidence:** Miedema, Fletcher & Aivaloglou 2022, ICPC, 'So many brackets!' (learners create their own complexity through nesting); Shute et al. 2024, PVLDB 17(12) (inside-out data flow is a reason SQL is hard to learn); Taipalus & Seppanen 2020 TOCE item PV4 (step-by-step query formulation). No study compares placing CTEs before or after subqueries, so the ordering advice is expert judgement.
- **Recommendation (after verification):** Decide in slice 0 and record as an owner-decision ERRATA entry. Option A (recommended, but it touches the locked 7 levels / 46 concepts): move a minimal CTE-01 into level 3, before JOIN-03, with AGG-02 as its prerequisite, and make CTE-01 a prerequisite of SUBQ-02. The concept count is unchanged. Option B (no locked change): teach WITH inside JOIN-03 and exempt JOIN-03 and the level-3 cases from the prerequisite check. Either way, add a content check that rejects SUM(DISTINCT ...) as a JOIN-03 key.

### T-03: Every graded item is a blank-page write: no find-the-bug, predict-the-output or choose-the-query tasks

Final severity: **should-change** (reviewer: must-change; skeptic: downgrade). Challenges an owner decision: no.

- **Problem:** Debugging someone else's broken query is a real screen and interview format, and it is part of the app's own definition of job-ready. Reading and tracing build the mental model that writing depends on. They also give a learner starting at zero a pretest they can actually attempt; at the moment they will fail two blank-editor pretests per concept for weeks and learn to skip them. And the item pools are less varied without them (see T-09).
- **Evidence:** Lopez, Whalley, Robbins & Lister 2008, ICER (reading and tracing relate to writing); Xie et al. 2019, Computer Science Education 29(2-3) (read before write); Sentance, Waite & Kallia 2019, PRIMM (Predict-Run-Investigate-Modify-Make) beat a control group across 13 schools; Taipalus & Seppanen 2020 TOCE items NA5 and NA8 (show erroneous queries and have students explain them; do not assess by code alone); Ericson, Margulieux & Rick 2017 and Wu & Ericson 2024 CHI (Parsons problems, SQL micro-Parsons); Pan & Carpenter 2023 (pretesting effects); 01 QS-06 and JR-05.
- **Recommendation (after verification):** Add the fix-this-query kind: slice 1 if it really is just a prefilled editor, otherwise slice 2 with the fan-out traps, where it also makes JR-05 computable. Store each starter query as prompt content of its own item, never reused from another item's keys. Add predict (row count or result) and choose-the-query items in slice 2, on the multiple-choice UI built for CP1/CP5. These new kinds feed lessons, mixed practice and scheduling, but only blank-editor write items count toward Mastered. Keep the pretest as is, or make one item a predict item once multiple choice exists. Parsons problems: post-v1.

### T-04: 'Show answer' before any attempt has two contradictory ratings, and the answer is not used as a worked example

Final severity: **must-change** (reviewer: must-change; skeptic: uphold). Challenges an owner decision: no.

- **Problem:** If the rule turns out to be 'rates nothing', the learner can open a due review, peek at the answer, close it and get a fresh item at no cost. The card then reads as retained although nothing was retrieved, which is exactly the shallow help use that goes with poor learning. Separately, an answer works as a worked example only if the learner processes it and then solves a similar problem soon after; a bare reveal wastes that.
- **Evidence:** The contradiction comes from reading the spec directly. Baker et al. 2004 CHI and Aleven et al. 2016 IJAIED (help abuse goes with lower learning); Palazzo et al. 2010 (copying homework answers correlated with about a 2 SD relative drop on similar exam problems); van Gog, Kester & Paas 2011 (an example followed by a similar problem beats problem solving alone); Shih, Koedinger & Scheines 2008 (bottom-out hints act as worked examples when they are processed).
- **Recommendation (after verification):** Write one rule into §5. (a) If the concept's card exists, a reveal or hint level 2 or more before any graded attempt rates Again when the instance closes, flagged viewed_before_attempt. (b) If no card exists yet (first exposure), it rates nothing and counts as a worked example. (c) Viewing the reference after a pass is free. (d) Reword line 228: after a reveal, the learner may still submit within the same instance for practice, but the instance rating stays Again. State that the 15-minute relearning step brings a sibling item of that concept back later in the session. Report the weekly viewed_before_attempt rate in the tune-up. Subgoal-labelled reveals depend on T-05 and are optional. The confirm-before-reveal nudge stays optional.

### T-05: Fading runs on one example per concept, so the early stages are retyping, not retrieval

Final severity: **should-change** (reviewer: should-change; skeptic: uphold). Challenges an owner decision: no.

- **Problem:** With one example faded in place, stage 1 asks the learner to retype the clause they read seconds earlier, and stage 2 the two clauses they read. That is copying from working memory, not retrieval, and because the learner moves up a stage after 1 correct answer, the stages advance on false evidence. In the research, fading runs across a sequence of different problems with the same structure. Without names for the steps, a novice also cannot see the reusable plan behind the example.
- **Evidence:** Renkl, Atkinson, Maier & Staley 2002, Journal of Experimental Education; Atkinson, Renkl & Merrill 2003, Journal of Educational Psychology 95(4). Subgoal labels: Margulieux, Guzdial & Catrambone 2012 ICER; Morrison, Margulieux & Guzdial 2015 ICER; Margulieux, Morrison & Decker 2020, International Journal of STEM Education. Evidence on self-explanation prompts is mixed (Barbieri et al. 2023, Educational Psychology Review), so keep any such prompt to one click. Akhuseyinoglu et al. 2022 ITiCSE (SQL worked examples; correlational).
- **Recommendation (after verification):** Clarify §4: stage 0 is the worked example, and stages 1-3 are different lesson-block items served with their existing fading boundaries. The item budget does not grow. In slice 0, add a fixed subgoal-label field to the item and worked-example schema, in evaluation order: source and grain, row filter, output grain, metrics, group filter, sort and limit. Reuse it in hint 1. Restore 'why this clause' as one optional one-click multiple-choice question when multiple choice exists, or record its removal as an owner override. The evidence on self-explanation prompts is mixed (Barbieri 2023).

### T-06: Grain, keys and evaluation order are never taught as mental models

Final severity: **should-change** (reviewer: should-change; skeptic: uphold). Challenges an owner decision: no.

- **Problem:** These three ideas sit under almost every logical error the app diagnoses: wrong grain (ERR-LOG-07), DISTINCT used as a fix-all, join fan-out, WHERE versus HAVING, and later 'why can't I filter a window in WHERE?'. Novices who do not know what a primary key implies pick tables by their column names and add DISTINCT on keys. Waiting until level 3 means two weeks of GROUP BY practice with no words for 'one row per what'. The case checkpoint CP1 (grain, denominator, which table) also assumes this vocabulary.
- **Evidence:** Miedema, Aivaloglou & Fletcher 2021 ICER (think-aloud study: confusion about what primary keys imply, choosing tables by column names, the DBMS as a black box); Taipalus 2020, Journal of Systems and Software 165:110576 (database complexity and schema representation affect query success); Shute et al. 2024 PVLDB (written clause order versus evaluation order); Evans 2019 (practitioner); Taipalus & Seppanen 2020 TOCE items DB10-DB14 and PV4-PV5; Akhuseyinoglu et al. 2022 (stepping through execution; correlational).
- **Recommendation (after verification):** Slice 1: show a one-line grain, the primary key and foreign keys with 1:N labels for every table and beginner view in the schema panel, generated from 05. Add an evaluation-order card to AGG-03, reused at WIN-06, that says DuckDB accepts aliases in WHERE but PostgreSQL rejects them. Teach 'what is one row here?' through the reading and the 'source and grain' subgoal in slice 1, and add multiple-choice 'which table / is this unique' items in slice 2, when multiple choice exists. Slice 2: a keys and diagram reading inside JOIN-01. Show input-table row counts (static) and the result row count. Drop row counts before and after each join step, which would need query instrumentation.

### T-07: DuckDB-only habits and silent integer-division bugs go unremarked on the learner's queries

Final severity: **should-change** (reviewer: should-change; skeptic: downgrade). Challenges an owner decision: no.

- **Problem:** Every habit forms on DuckDB, but the screens Aydin will sit run other engines. HackerRank offers SQL Server, MySQL, PostgreSQL, DB2 and Oracle and scores on exact output; CoderPad runs MySQL or PostgreSQL. An alias in WHERE is an error there. A conversion rate computed as integer divided by integer silently returns 0 in PostgreSQL and SQL Server, and ratios like that are exactly what a pricing or marketing analyst gets tested on. The spec's own JR-04 ('All ratio problems correct under both DuckDB and PostgreSQL division rules') has no mechanism behind it.
- **Evidence:** DuckDB documentation, Friendly SQL page, checked 2026-10-02: it lists aliases in WHERE/GROUP BY/HAVING, GROUP BY ALL, ORDER BY ALL, FROM-first, EXCLUDE/REPLACE, count() and trailing commas. DuckDB 0.8.0 announcement (float '/'); HackerRank candidate support (exact string matching, choice of dialect); CoderPad interview database docs; Taipalus & Seppanen 2020 TOCE (teach standard SQL, or use two DBMSs); 01 DIALECT-01, REC-05, JR-04 and JR-17; review item 3 (== and aliases accepted).
- **Recommendation (after verification):** Slice 1: integer-division trap items and dialect notes in TYPE-01 and AGG-04, which is content only. Add a notes-only lint for alias references in WHERE/GROUP BY/HAVING and for ==. DuckDB's json_serialize_sql parse tree plus schema metadata can drive it. Add a spike part A probe: can a second locked runner be created with integer_division=true? If yes, slice 2 re-runs passing queries that contain '/' there and shows an exact portability note. If no, the trap items are enough. Extend the lint (GROUP BY ALL, FROM-first, EXCLUDE, count(), trailing commas, :: casts, ILIKE, QUALIFY at level 5) when those constructs become teachable or when the log shows them being used.

### T-08: Mastery can be reached with one blank-editor solve, and 'I was right' can create mastery

Final severity: **should-change** (reviewer: should-change; skeptic: downgrade). Challenges an owner decision: yes.

- **Problem:** A concrete path: on day 1 the learner solves the stage-1 and stage-2 faded items on the first try, writing one clause each; on day 2 they pass one easy (E1) review. The concept is now Mastered after a single blank-editor solve, about 24 hours after first contact. Performance in blocked, same-day practice overstates learning, and three easy successes give many false positives on transfer problems. Mastered drives level completion, the progress view and Aydin's sense of readiness for the December screens. The override hole contradicts the project's own premise that Aydin cannot yet tell a wrong key from a real mistake. A novice who believes their query is equivalent can turn a misconception into a mastered concept.
- **Evidence:** Soderstrom & Bjork 2015, Perspectives on Psychological Science (learning versus performance); Rohrer & Taylor 2007 and Rohrer et al. 2020 (blocked practice inflates practice scores); Kelly, Wang, Thompson & Heffernan 2015 EDM (3 correct in a row: 16 of 33 students then failed the transfer item); Rawson & Dunlosky 2011, Journal of Experimental Psychology: General (reach a criterion, then relearn across spaced sessions); Dunlosky & Rawson 2012 (overconfidence produces underachievement); 08 PF-03 ('Mastery requires a blank-editor solve'); aydinlearns/CLAUDE.md non-negotiable 6.
- **Recommendation (after verification):** Adopt now (§22 and clarification). 'I was right' schedules as Hard at once, but counts toward Practised and Mastered only after the weekly review confirms it, and the learner clicks the diff row they dispute. Faded-stage attempts count as assisted: they count toward Practised, never toward Mastered. This matters if T-05 is adopted. Offer the owner, as an optional refinement of the locked rule with no strong evidence for the exact thresholds: at most 1 of the 3 solves may come from the day-1 lesson block, and at least 1 should be on an E2/E3 item or a different table.

### T-09: No rule says which difficulty or sub-skill each block draws, and the review pools will run dry

Final severity: **should-change** (reviewer: should-change; skeptic: uphold). Challenges an owner decision: no.

- **Problem:** A random E3 on a review day turns a concept the learner knows into an Again and shortens its interval. A lesson block that opens with an E3 is a cliff for a beginner. With bundled concepts, half of a card's content (BETWEEN with half-open ranges, say) may never come up in review. A concept in its first month gets roughly 8-14 servings across mixed practice, reviews, re-tests and mistake cards, so a 6-item pool starts repeating within about two weeks, and the learner starts recognising answers instead of retrieving them.
- **Evidence:** 02 RULE-16 (a design heuristic from desirable-difficulty research); Hausknecht et al. 2007, Journal of Applied Psychology (retest gains are larger on identical forms than on alternate forms); Pan & Rickard 2018, Psychological Bulletin (transfer is weaker than gains on the same items); Matuschak (practitioner notes on varied prompts); Choffin et al. 2019, DAS3H (item difficulty matters when scheduling skills). The 8-14 servings figure is my estimate.
- **Recommendation (after verification):** Add to §4 and §11. Fallback: when no unseen item exists, serve the least recently seen one and flag it repeat_exposure; it counts for scheduling but not for mastery. Band policy: the lesson block runs E1 then E2. Re-tests and reviews draw E2 (E1 during a concept's first week). Mixed practice, drills and cases may draw E3. Tag sub-skills on bundled concepts and rotate reviews across them. Raise pools to 10-12 items for the most-served concepts. Record RULE-16 as adopted in this simpler form, or as an owner override.

### T-10: Item prompts have no style guide by level, the learner never sees the expected output shape, and no blind-solver check runs on SQL items

Final severity: **should-change** (reviewer: should-change; skeptic: uphold). Challenges an owner decision: no.

- **Problem:** Ambiguous prompts are a major cause of 'wrong answer, but why?', and this learner will read every such failure as their own mistake. Beginners find it hardest to know when GROUP BY is needed, so early prompts should cue it and later ones should not. Realistic manager vagueness belongs at levels 5-6 and in cases, not in level 1. An output contract (columns, types, one row per what) is what LeetCode and DataLemur show, and it costs almost nothing.
- **Evidence:** Taipalus & Seppanen 2020 TOCE items DB3-DB9 (clearer task statements mean fewer attempts, fewer errors and less time; ambiguity should rise as training goes on); Poulsen et al. 2020 ITiCSE, summarising Reisner (the hard part of GROUP BY is knowing when to use it); 08 PF-02 (opaque wrong answers); DataLemur and LeetCode show example output before submission (industry practice).
- **Recommendation (after verification):** Slice 0: a one-page prompt style guide by level (explicit 'for each X' cues and named output columns at levels 1-2, fading by levels 3-4, manager wording at levels 5-6 and in cases). Slice 1: an output contract above the editor (column names, type classes, 'one row per X'), left off openers and CP1. Add a blind-solver content check for SQL items: an agent solves from the prompt and schema alone, the grader compares its result with the key, and any mismatch fails the item until the prompt is reworded. The expected row count waits for an owner ruling under the three-case key rule.

### T-11: Feedback says nothing on a pass, the partial score misleads, and hidden-data failures show whole tables

Final severity: **consider** (reviewer: should-change; skeptic: downgrade). Challenges an owner decision: no.

- **Problem:** These are three gaps a teacher would close. A pass with no 'why this works' leaves lucky or half-understood solutions unconsolidated. A single number such as 80/100, on a query that fails only the NULL case, reads as 'nearly right', although the business answer is wrong. Whole edge-case tables bury the one row that matters, and shown after every failure they teach the hidden data rather than the concept. 02's templates also describe the symptom more than the misconception behind it.
- **Evidence:** Mitrovic, Ohlsson & Barrow 2013, Computers & Education (adding positive feedback to SQL-Tutor roughly halved learning time); Van der Kleij et al. 2015, Review of Educational Research (explanatory feedback beats being shown the correct answer, which beats right/wrong alone); Koenka et al. 2021 (grades alone lowered motivation compared with comments); Miao, Roy & Yang 2019 SIGMOD, RATest (small counterexamples explain wrong queries); Miedema et al. 2021 and 2022 (feedback that targets misconceptions; refutation texts); Butterfield & Metcalfe 2001 (high-confidence errors are corrected more readily after feedback).
- **Recommendation (after verification):** Cheap and worth doing in slice 1: store a one-line 'why this works' per item (generated with the package) and show it on a pass. Show the partial score as a Shape / Grain / Values / Edge cases checklist on the result screen, keeping the single number for level and progress views. Instead of provenance, give each edge-case schema a short list of what it contains (NULL channel rows, ties, empty group) and show that list with the capped diff. Using the confidence rating to order the wrap-up's mistake step can wait for slice 2.

### T-12: Show the item's alternative correct solutions after a pass

Final severity: **consider** (reviewer: should-change; skeptic: downgrade). Challenges an owner decision: yes.

- **Problem:** Comparing correct methods side by side builds flexibility: COUNT(*) FILTER versus SUM(CASE ...), a CTE versus a derived table, NOT EXISTS versus a LEFT JOIN anti-join. Interviewers and take-home reviewers probe exactly this ('Can you do it another way?'), and reading other people's SQL is a daily part of the job. The content already exists and has already passed the checks.
- **Evidence:** Rittle-Johnson & Star 2007, Journal of Educational Psychology 99(3) (comparing solution methods improved procedural flexibility and transfer; randomised trial, algebra); Patitsas, Craig & Easterbrook 2013 ICER (comparing and contrasting algorithms in computer science); Codewars and DataLemur show other solutions after completion (industry practice); 08 SCR-04.
- **Recommendation (after verification):** Ask the owner, batched with the other key-rule questions: allow a fourth logged case, 'Other ways to write this', after an instance closes with a pass (in drills and mocks, at the end-of-run review), with no rating effect. If approved, ship it in slice 2. The generator then marks one alternative as a genuinely different method and adds a one-line trade-off. The panel is hidden for items whose alternatives are only cosmetic.

### T-13: Interleaving can be undone by visible labels and per-lesson autocomplete, and the registry misses common beginner confusions

Final severity: **should-change** (reviewer: should-change; skeptic: downgrade). Challenges an owner decision: no.

- **Problem:** Interleaving works because the learner has to decide from the problem itself which approach it needs. A visible 'HAVING' label, or autocomplete that offers only the two tables the item needs, makes that decision for the learner and turns mixed practice back into blocked practice. Several of the most common beginner confusions are missing from the registry, so slice 1's mixed practice trains too little discrimination.
- **Evidence:** Rohrer 2012, Educational Psychology Review (interleaving helps because the learner must choose the strategy); Brunmair & Richter 2019, Psychological Bulletin (g = 0.42, larger when categories are similar); Taipalus et al. 2018 TOCE and 02 ERR-LOG-03 and -13 (common novice errors).
- **Recommendation (after verification):** Slice 1 UI rule: in reviews, mixed practice, drills and cases, hide the concept name, lesson title and level badge until after submission, and do not highlight the tables an item needs. From level 3, when table choice becomes the skill, make autocomplete cover the whole company schema outside the lesson block. Add only cross-concept pairs to the registry now: DISTINCT vs GROUP BY (SORT-01/AGG-02) and WHERE-filter vs CASE-label (FILTER-01/CASE-01). Add the later ones when their levels ship: GROUP BY vs PARTITION BY, self-join vs LAG, WHERE vs QUALIFY or a CTE, IN-subquery vs JOIN. Intra-concept confusions stay as trap items.

### T-14: Planning, self-checking and explaining a query only arrive at level 7, after applications start

Final severity: **consider** (reviewer: should-change; skeptic: downgrade). Challenges an owner decision: no.

- **Problem:** Applications start in December, so the first screens come before level 7 exists. NL hiring funnels open with a timed online SQL test, then move to case rounds and live SQL where the candidate talks while typing; grain and metric definitions matter more than syntax there. Practising under the conditions you will be tested in transfers better, and planning before writing reduces errors. These habits take weeks to form, so they have to start with the first cases, not after the deadline.
- **Evidence:** Morris, Bransford & Franks 1977 (transfer-appropriate processing); Taipalus & Seppanen 2020 TOCE item PV2 (planning reduces errors); 01 §1.2 (NL hiring funnels), QS-01 to QS-03, JR-15 and JR-16; DataLemur SQL interview guide (clarify the question, consider edge cases, test step by step; industry practice); HackerRank candidate support (exact output).
- **Recommendation (after verification):** Low-cost version: in slice 2, extend CP1 with an optional typed plan in CRAFT-03's template fields (output grain, tables and keys, filters, metric, expected row count), and show the predicted row count next to the actual one after CP3. After level 3, add a 'screen mini mock' drill preset (4-5 mixed problems, 30-40 minutes, no hints, keyword-only autocomplete, require_rounding on). Defer 'explain to the manager' self-checks and the multi-step mock variant until after slice 2 lands, if the pace allows.

### T-15: Levels 1-2 run on dimension tables only, so beginner practice will not feel like pricing work

Final severity: **should-change** (reviewer: should-change; skeptic: downgrade). Challenges an owner decision: no.

- **Problem:** Those four tables are all dimensions: they hold no units, revenue, orders or sale dates, and Voltmarkt's products table does not even carry a list price (it lives in price_history). A pricing or commercial analyst's first questions are revenue per category, units per store, margin %, and the share sold on promotion. Level 2's exit criterion (a per-segment scorecard computed from sums, not from averaged ratios) and the averaging-ratios trap both need a fact table with numerators and denominators. Two to three weeks of GROUP BY on product counts per brand will feel like a textbook, not a job, and 01's level 1-2 patterns already need columns those tables lack (planning read issue 3).
- **Evidence:** Expert judgement; 01 LVL-02 exit criterion and AGG-01 E3 (overall CTR as SUM/SUM versus the naive average); planning read issue 3; 05 FIND-01-02 ('TVs lead revenue, cables lead units'), a natural level 2 finding that needs sales data; Taipalus 2020, Journal of Systems and Software (simpler schemas raise success, which favours one wide table early).
- **Recommendation (after verification):** Slice 0, with the Voltmarkt generator changes: define one canonical beginner view at order-line grain (order date, week and month columns, store, country, channel, category, product, units, net revenue, unit cost; no order-level measures, so there is no fan-out). Give it a stated grain in the schema panel and its own edge schema. Use it for level 2 aggregation items, the averaging-ratios traps and the level 2 opener. Level 1 can stay on the small tables. Revisit the view at JOIN-01 as 'these four tables joined'.

### T-16: Ratings land on the target concept even when a different skill broke, and a heavily helped case pass still earns Good

Final severity: **should-change** (reviewer: should-change; skeptic: downgrade). Challenges an owner decision: no.

- **Problem:** A JOIN item failed because of WHERE versus HAVING puts the blame on JOIN-01: the review goes to the wrong skill, and the weakness in AGG-03 stays hidden, although the diagnosis already knows the error ID and which concept it belongs to. ## Edtech developer / learning engineer (LE-)

**Verdict.** The learning engine is better founded than most practice products. It has concept cards that serve a different item at each review, ratings computed as a pure function of an append-only log so they can be replayed, partial credit kept out of scheduling, a mastery rule stricter than N-in-a-row, and diagnosis based on planted wrong queries run on a hidden dataset. The weak point is what is allowed to feed the memory model and the progress signals. As written, two kinds of failure write permanent difficulty into FSRS cards: failures while still learning (a pretest on syntax not yet taught, the blocked lesson items) and failures on cards that are not due (mixed practice, drill time-outs). The reason is that in FSRS-6 a Good rating never lowers difficulty. With the default weights that means about 2.7 to 4 times the reviews per concept, and each SQL review takes minutes, not the seconds a flashcard takes. Two rules give conflicting ratings for 'show answer' and the intake guard is sized for flashcards. Several progress signals can mislead one learner on a deadline: Mastered can appear 24 hours after first contact, an override counts at once, a 10-item drill can be retaken from a 20-item pool, and the GA4 'pass probability' is built from lower bounds. Almost every fix is a rule in the rating mapper or the session composer, and replay applies those rules to past attempts as well, so none of this blocks the implementation plan. Settle the two must-change items in the slice 0 paper. Trim the GA4 readiness model and the pre-December optimiser work, which cost build time and buy little for one learner.

**Solid, keep:**
- One FSRS card per concept, with a different item at each review and a 30-day no-repeat rule (RULE-02). For skills this is the right mix: Aydin practises the skill, not a memorised answer.
- The rating is a pure function of the logged raw outcomes, and learner state is rebuilt by replaying the append-only log (§5 l.226-227, §12 l.977-984). Every rating, mastery or composer policy can be changed later and applied retroactively with no data loss. This is the single most valuable design decision in the engine; protect it.
- Partial credit is shown and logged but never changes a rating or mastery (§3 l.88, §5 l.280-281). A near-miss treated as recalled would schedule the review too late.
- The mastery rule needs varied items, two or more calendar days, at least one interleaved solve and no mistake card in relearning. That is stronger than N-correct-in-a-row, which the knowledge-tracing literature shows gives false positives on transfer.
- The graded-attempt definition is sound: a 60-second grace for syntax slips, time-outs graded, runner crashes not graded. So is the one-rating-per-item-instance unit, recorded in an item_close record. It gives clean units for analysis.
- Instruction comes first in a lesson (at most 5 minutes of reading), followed by an adaptively faded worked example and a pretest skip. This matches the worked-example and expertise-reversal evidence for new syntax with many interacting parts. Do not reorder lessons to put problems first.
- Blocked first exposure, then interleaving driven by a registry of look-alike concepts, matches the interleaving meta-analysis (larger effects for similar categories).
- Diagnosis comes from planted wrong queries mapped to error IDs and run on a hidden edge-case dataset, and a content check that every planted wrong query is caught. This is XData-style, the best-evidenced way to avoid false positives and to name the misconception.
- Every FSRS preset value is set explicitly: 15-minute steps, 180-day cap, fuzz on, per deck. The UTC versus Amsterdam day boundary is handled explicitly.
- Demotion and leech work as flags, never as locks. Self-checked items drive scheduling but not mastery.
- No streaks. 'Nothing is locked', with the recommended session as the default, is defensible for a motivated adult: learner control averages out near zero, and the default keeps most of the benefit of model-driven sequencing.
- The GA4 held-out mock pool is reserved before any drilling, with enemy groups and the answer-length content check. That is real exposure control.

### LE-01: Failures while learning write permanent difficulty into the concept card

Final severity: **should-change** (reviewer: must-change; skeptic: downgrade). Challenges an owner decision: no.

- **Problem:** In FSRS-6 (ts-fsrs 5.4.2), difficulty changes by -w6*(G-3), with linear damping and a mean reversion weight of w7 = 0.001. A Good leaves difficulty unchanged. Only Easy lowers it, and only a little: from 8.7 one Easy brings it to about 8.3. Easy is also rare here (reviews only, within 0.5x the target time). Again and Hard raise difficulty. The first rating sets the starting difficulty: about 2.1 for Good, 6.4 for Again. So a zero-SQL learner who fails the optional pretest on syntax never taught, or misses one blocked lesson item, ends up with a card marked hard for the rest of the course. Its stability then grows 2.5 to 5 times more slowly, through the (11-D) term. Failure while learning is expected and is useful for diagnosis, but it says nothing about memory. Each SQL review costs minutes, so the extra reviews come straight out of the 10 h/week.
- **Evidence:** FSRS formulas read from ts-fsrs v5.4.2 packages/fsrs/src/algorithm.ts (fetched 2026-10-02). Default weights from 02 l.243. My deterministic simulation used the 21 defaults at 0.90 retention, with every later review rated Good, the interval equal to stability (minimum 1 day), a 120-day horizon, and no fuzz or lapses, so real counts are higher. Results: first rating a post-lesson Good gives D 2.1 and 3 reviews (intervals 2, 12 and 50 days). Two pretest Agains, then all Goods, give D 8.7 and 8 reviews (1, 1, 1, 3, 6, 11, 19 and 34 days). Two pretest Agains plus one lesson Again give D 9.5 and 12 reviews. One Hard for slow time gives D 4.7 and 4 reviews. Rough estimate: if two failures while learning are typical, that is on the order of 10-15 extra hours across 46 concepts, out of the 128-hour SQL path. Soderstrom & Bjork 2015: performance during learning is not a measure of retention. Pan & Carpenter 2023: pretests on material not yet taught are expected to fail.
- **Recommendation (after verification):** Put this in the slice 0 rating-mapper rules, in the same block as LE-02 and LE-03, and amend l.196-197. Pretest failures and lesson-phase attempts (faded stages, lesson block) write no card review, but they still count toward Practised and the error log. The card's first rating is the first graded retrieval at least 15 minutes after first exposure; in the recommended path that is the RULE-08 re-test. A pretest passed without help creates the card with Good. Add the fallback the recommendation is missing: if a concept had lesson activity but no qualifying retrieval by the end of the session (the learner left early, or free study), create the card at session end with no rating, due on the next study day (ts-fsrs createEmptyCard with a due date), so the concept cannot drop out of the schedule. Add a scheduler test that a failed pretest creates no rating.

### LE-02: 'Show answer' before any attempt has two conflicting rules, and one reading breaks the owner's own rule

Final severity: **must-change** (reviewer: must-change; skeptic: uphold). Challenges an owner decision: no.

- **Problem:** If such a peek rates nothing, Aydin can open a due review, peek, leave, get a fresh item next time and peek again. The card never records the missed retrieval and stays due forever. That reading also contradicts the locked navigation row, which says show answer 'lowers that item's rating' (l.83). A bottom-out answer only teaches when the learner studies it and then solves a similar problem, and the spec never schedules that problem.
- **Evidence:** Internal contradiction in the spec. Van Gog, Kester & Paas 2011: an example followed by a similar problem beats problem solving alone, and solving first then seeing the example did not. Shih, Koedinger & Scheines 2008: bottom-out hints can work as worked examples. Palazzo et al. 2010: copying answers correlated with about a 2 SD relative drop on similar exam problems.

Log it with close reason show_answer and no submissions; viewed-before-attempt can then be derived, so no extra flag is needed. (2) Before the card's first rating (a new concept, see LE-01): rates nothing and counts as a worked example. (3) Viewing the reference after a pass is free. (4) Settle l.222 against l.228. Recommendation: the instance stays open after show answer so the learner can type and run the query; any pass in it rates Again and never counts toward Practised or Mastered. (5) Add no new composer mechanism. An Again already puts the card into its 15-minute relearning step, so just state that due learning and relearning cards are served again in the same session with a fresh item. Per-clause notes on the reference are optional; reuse the hint text rather than writing a new field for 160 items.

### LE-03: Failures in mixed practice, drills and cases become lapses, demotions and leeches

Final severity: **should-change** (reviewer: should-change; skeptic: downgrade). Challenges an owner decision: no.

- **Problem:** Interleaved practice and trap items are meant to lower performance in the session; that is the desirable difficulty. Yet each failure on a card that is not due counts as an FSRS lapse. It raises difficulty permanently (the mechanism in LE-01) and counts toward demotion and leech. In FSRS terms 'Again' also covers passes on attempt 3 and passes after hint 2. So one hard timed drill can demote a mastered concept. The case rule has the opposite bias: it credits Good after a struggle. Either way, the card's state stops meaning 'how well I remember this'.
- **Evidence:** Simulation, same method as LE-01: one mixed-practice failure the day after a clean start, on a card not yet due, raises D from 2.1 to 7.4 and the 120-day review count from 3 to 5. Rohrer & Taylor 2007 and Brunmair & Richter 2019: interleaved practice scores are lower even when learning is better. Soderstrom & Bjork 2015 and Kornell & Bjork 2008 make the same point. The policy below is expert judgement.
- **Recommendation (after verification):** Keep rating failures on cards that are not due, and make three narrow fixes. (1) Cases: apply the normal rating map to each checkpoint. First attempt without help gives Good, otherwise Hard; after show answer, concepts that were not diagnosed get no credit; the diagnosed concept gets Again. (2) Within one drill run or one mixed-practice block, rate a card at most once, using the worst outcome, so one block cannot stack Agains. (3) Demotion needs its 2 Agains on 2 different days. Leave the leech rule on FSRS lapses.

### LE-04: A concept can reach Mastered about 24 hours after first contact

Final severity: **should-change** (reviewer: should-change; skeptic: downgrade). Challenges an owner decision: yes.

- **Problem:** Two blocked lesson-block solves on day 1 plus one mixed-practice solve on day 2 meet the rule, so 'Mastered' can appear 24 hours after first contact, built mostly on blocked practice, which overstates learning. Because solves are cumulative, a learner who passes about half the time also collects 3 qualifying solves eventually. All three can be easy E1 variants. Level completion, the progress view and the job-ready criteria all read this signal, and with a December deadline a false 'mastered' does more harm than a late one.
- **Evidence:** Soderstrom & Bjork 2015. Rohrer & Taylor 2007 and Rohrer et al. 2020 (delayed d = 0.83): blocked practice overstates learning. Kelly et al. 2015, EDM: 3 in a row predicted the next item about 80% of the time, but 16 of 33 students who reached it failed the transfer item (small sample). Rawson & Dunlosky 2011: reaching a criterion and then relearning in spaced sessions. Pelánek & Řihák 2017: the threshold and the data matter more than the model.
- **Recommendation (after verification):** Take it to the owner as a clarification of which solves count, recommending two changes. (a) Pretest, lesson-block and re-test solves do not qualify. (c) The 3 qualifying solves must be among the concept's last 4 graded first attempts. That stops a learner who passes half the time from accumulating mastery, and stops a concept flipping between mastered and demoted. Drop the 3-day rule and the E2/E3 requirement for now. Both changes are pure functions of the log, so replay applies them.

### LE-05: 'I was right' lets the novice's own judgement mint mastery

Final severity: **should-change** (reviewer: should-change; skeptic: uphold). Challenges an owner decision: no.

- **Problem:** The project's own premise is that Aydin 'cannot yet tell a wrong key from a real mistake'. Novices are systematically overconfident. An override that counts toward Mastered at once lets the main progress signal rest on that judgement for up to a week, and a habit of overriding is cheap to form. The override itself is right, as a safety valve against grader false negatives. This changes a §22 proposal, not a locked row.

Mastered needs 3 different items, so an unconfirmed override costs only one extra solve on another item, and the early weeks need no special path. Optional: ask the learner to click the diff row they think is wrong. Track the override rate per item as an item-health signal.

### LE-06: The intake guard is sized for flashcards

Final severity: **consider** (reviewer: should-change; skeptic: downgrade). Challenges an owner decision: no.

### LE-07: Item pools run dry, and review items vary too much in difficulty

Final severity: **should-change** (reviewer: should-change; skeptic: downgrade). Challenges an owner decision: no.

- **Problem:** On a clean path a concept gets about 2 reviews in its first 30 days, plus 3-5 mixed-practice servings. On a struggling path (intervals of 1, 1, 1, 3, 6 and 11 days) it gets 6 reviews alone, plus mistake-card servings. A pool of 6 runs dry in 1-2 weeks for exactly the concepts that need the most practice, and the composer's behaviour is then undefined. Repeating identical items inflates scores, so repeats must not count as evidence. And because one card covers E1 to E3 items, an Again on an unusually hard variant shortens the interval for the whole concept, so item difficulty becomes noise in the card.
- **Evidence:** Hausknecht et al. 2007: retest gains are larger on identical forms than on alternate forms. Pan & Rickard 2018: transfer is weaker than the gain on the same items. Choffin et al. 2019 (DAS3H): modelling item difficulty improves prediction. Planning issue 25: RULE-16's difficulty changes confound per-card difficulty. Intervals from the LE-01 simulation.
- **Recommendation (after verification):** (a) Fallback: serve the concept's least recently seen item and flag it repeat_exposure. It rates the card but never counts toward Mastered. (b) Optional in slice 1, recommended by slice 2: fix-this-query variants. The editor is pre-filled with a planted wrong query and graded with the same key and grader. Use them for at most about 1 in 3 review servings, and do not count them toward Mastered. (c) Prefer E2 items for scheduled reviews when one is available, but never filter the pool down to E2. Drop (d). Have the tune-up flag concepts that hit the fallback often, and generate extra items only for those.

### LE-08: Mistake cards are never retired and can arrive in a burst

Final severity: **consider** (reviewer: should-change; skeptic: downgrade). Challenges an owner decision: no.

- **Problem:** Without retirement, the first two errors per concept hold the cap forever, and later, more important errors never get a card. A mistake card that draws ordinary items is a second concept card, so the same concept is scheduled twice. Seeding on the day slice 2 lands can create up to 24 cards at once (12 concepts x 2), a review spike in the same week joins arrive.
- **Evidence:** 02 RULE-03 and RULE-20. Expert judgement.

### LE-09: Drills and the job-ready mock are too small and too repeatable to support a pass/fail decision

Final severity: **consider** (reviewer: should-change; skeptic: downgrade). Challenges an owner decision: no.

- **Problem:** Reliability: with 10 items at 90%, a learner whose true item success is 80% passes once 38% of the time, and within three tries 76%. A true-90% learner fails 26% of single attempts. With a 20-item pool, the second and third attempts are mostly items already seen, so a pass increasingly measures recall of those items. The 8-item mock passes a true-65% learner 43% of the time and fails a true-80% learner 20% of the time. Learning transfers to unseen problems less well than it shows on practised ones, and every in-app item comes from one authoring process and one data world. In-app pass marks will therefore overstate readiness for employer screens.
- **Evidence:** My binomial calculation. Hausknecht et al. 2007 (retest effects). Pan & Rickard 2018 (transfer d = 0.40, weaker than the gain on the same items).
- **Recommendation (after verification):** Now: add a monthly outside check, for example LeetCode SQL 50 in PostgreSQL mode or HackerRank's SQL (Intermediate) assessment, logged as a manual event or just noted in the tune-up. Level drills: show the score history, and count a pass toward completion only when most of its items were unseen in the last 30 days. Grow drill pools from 20 to about 30 only if the templates make it cheap. Decide on parallel mock forms and the margin wording when slice 6 is planned. Drill ratings follow LE-03's once-per-block rule.

### LE-10: GA4: the readiness gate cannot be met, and there is no multiple-choice rating rule

Final severity: **should-change** (reviewer: should-change; skeptic: uphold). Challenges an owner decision: no.

- **Problem:** Putting lower bounds into a pass probability does not give a probability. At a per-item success of 0.72, a typical topic lower bound, the chance of 40 or more out of 50 is 0.13. A learner whose true rate is 0.88 passes 97% of the time. So the gate stays shut for a learner who is actually ready, or it gets quietly tuned until it opens, and then it is a number with no ground truth. With about 30 or more GA4 concepts and a few cold attempts each by late November, the concept posteriors stay wide: 4 of 5 correct gives a 5% lower bound of 0.42, and 16 of 20 gives 0.62. The exam is free, the pass mark is 80%, attempts are unlimited and a fail costs a 24-hour wait, so the gate guards a cheap event, and slice 3b's growth from 113 to 200 items is the expensive part of the GA4 build. Without a multiple-choice rule, a lucky guess (25% chance) is rated like a known answer.
- **Evidence:** My binomial and Beta-quantile arithmetic. Google Skillshop FAQ: 80% pass mark, 24-hour wait, unlimited attempts (from the edtech digest). 06 l.568 sets its own readiness rule: two full mocks at 90% or more. The planning read notes that 06's topic weights are estimates. Expert judgement.
- **Recommendation (after verification):** (1) Replace the Beta model with a readable gate: one realistic mock (or two half-mocks) on unseen held-out items at 85% or more, plus every topic at 75% or more on cold attempts, with the attempt counts shown. Then sit the exam. (2) Keep slice 3's 50 held-out items, and grow the bank to about 200 only if a half-mock shows topic gaps. (3) Define GA4 cards: recommended one per 06 concept, with 10's concepts rated on their parent and the same pool-exhaustion fallback as LE-07. (4) Multiple-choice rating map: a wrong answer, or opening the explanation before answering, rates Again; a correct answer at confidence 1-2 rates Hard; any other correct answer rates Good; Easy is never used. Change (1) and (2) in §8 and §15 now; settle (3) and (4) before slice 3.

### LE-12: The content checks cannot catch an ambiguous prompt, the error a novice is least able to spot

Final severity: **should-change** (reviewer: should-change; skeptic: uphold). Challenges an owner decision: no.

- **Problem:** Every SQL check compares the key with itself or with the generator's own alternative solutions, which share the generator's reading of the prompt. When a prompt can reasonably mean something else (one row per what, whether NULL categories count, rounding, sort), a correct learner answer fails. For a novice this is the most damaging error. It teaches the wrong lesson, writes a false lapse into FSRS and creates a false mistake card, and it erodes trust in the feedback. Non-negotiable 6 says Aydin cannot catch it. With about 160 items generated in a week, an ambiguity rate of even 3-5% means 5-8 bad items in slice 1.
- **Evidence:** Taipalus & Seppänen 2020, TOCE, items DB3-DB9: ambiguous task statements lead to more attempts and errors. Wang et al. 2024: deceptive errors and false positives in online judges. Poulsen et al. 2020: novices struggle to know when GROUP BY is needed. The 3-5% rate is my assumption.
- **Recommendation (after verification):** (1) In slice 0, write a prompt style guide for levels 1-2: name the output columns and state the grain, the rounding, the sort and how NULL rows are handled. Ambiguity rises on purpose from level 5. (2) Add a blind-solver step to the slice 1 content checks. An agent sees only the prompt, the schema and the rules badge, and its query runs through the app's grader. On a disagreement, an agent tightens the prompt and re-runs the check; nothing is queued for Aydin. An LLM solver may read a prompt the same way the generator did, so the style guide carries most of the load.

### LE-14: Interleaving loses its effect when items give away their concept, and level 1 has no look-alike pairs

Final severity: **should-change** (reviewer: should-change; skeptic: downgrade). Challenges an owner decision: no.

- **Problem:** Interleaving works mainly because the learner has to work out from the problem which approach it needs. A concept label, an autocomplete scoped to the lesson, or a schema panel that shows only the item's tables removes that step, and mixed practice quietly turns back into blocked practice. With no level 1 pairs there is little for mixed practice to interleave at level 1, yet that is where the '= NULL' and AND/OR precedence errors start.
- **Evidence:** Rohrer 2012: interleaving helps learners tell similar concepts apart. Brunmair & Richter 2019: larger effects for similar categories. Miedema, Aivaloglou & Fletcher 2021: misconceptions from natural language, such as using 'IS NOT' as an operator.
- **Recommendation (after verification):** Add one sentence to §13: in reviews, mixed practice and drills, the item shows no concept name, lesson title or level badge until after submission. Keep the schema panel and autocomplete as specified. Optionally add WHERE vs CASE in SELECT and DISTINCT vs GROUP BY to the registry for level 2.

### SQL education research and expert SQL teaching practice, applied as a methodology review of the aydinlearns v1 design spec (2026-10-01), fro

- **OVERALL VERDICT (reviewer's expert judgement, built from the sources in the findings below). The engine side of the spec is well grounded: result-set grading with a hidden edge-case dataset, diagnosis from planted wrong queries (XData-style), spacing and interleaving, a strict mastery rule, partial credit kept out of scheduling, on-demand hints that cost rating, and case-first framing. - **Parsons problems give the same learning gains as writing code in less time. Distractor blocks teach students to spot common errors. - **In the worked-example literature, fading runs across a sequence of different, structurally identical problems: a full example, then a new problem with one step blanked, and so on until the learner solves alone. - **Schema comprehension drives query success. Simpler databases give significantly higher success rates. A leading interview-prep source lists data modelling as one of five core areas that SQL interviews test.** (moderate (several studies)). - **SQL's written clause order (SELECT ... FROM ... WHERE ... GROUP BY) does not match its evaluation order (FROM/JOIN, WHERE, GROUP BY, HAVING, SELECT, ORDER BY, LIMIT). Google's GoogleSQL team names this mismatch, and the 'inside-out' data flow of nested queries, as reasons SQL is hard to learn. Practitioners teach evaluation order explicitly, and SQL step-through tools visualise it.** (weak (single study / expert opinion)). - **Grading against a single fixed dataset produces false positives. Mutation-based test data (XData) outperformed predefined datasets and manual TA grading. Showing a small counterexample database where the wrong query and the reference differ (RATest) was used successfully in a large course.** (moderate (several studies)). - **Feedback design. Adding positive feedback (why a correct step is correct) to SQL-Tutor made students learn at twice the speed of error-only feedback. SQL-Tutor's classroom evaluations found that high-level advice benefited learning most. Hints on demand produced more learning than hints given automatically after an error. Learners often misuse on-demand help. High-confidence errors are corrected more readily after feedback (hypercorrection).** (moderate (several studies)). - **Prompt wording matters. Less ambiguous task statements mean fewer attempts, fewer errors and less time. Early exercises that need GROUP BY should signal it clearly, and ambiguity should rise as training progresses, toward workplace realism. Giving learners the correct result table, or its row count, is recommended by some studies and opposed by others, because students may brute-force a query to fit it.** (moderate (several studies)). Sources: Taipalus & Seppänen 2020, ACM TOCE, items DB3-DB9 and DB15, doi:10.1145/3398377; Poulsen et al. 2020, ITiCSE (Reisner: the difficulty with GROUP BY is knowing when to use it)
- **Mastery criteria. In successive relearning, reaching a criterion and then relearning across spaced sessions produces durable retention. In ASSISTments, 3 consecutive correct answers predicted the next problem well (80% got the next two right), but a higher threshold (5) predicted transfer-question performance better.** (moderate (several studies)). Sources: Rawson & Dunlosky 2011, Journal of Experimental Psychology: General 140:283-302, 'Optimizing schedules of retrieval practice for durable and efficient learning: How much is enough?', doi:10.1037/a0023956; Kelly, Wang, Thompson, Heffernan 2015, EDM, 'Defining Mastery: Knowledge Tracing Versus N-Consecutive Correct Responses', https://www.educationaldatamining.org/EDM2015/proceedings/poster630-631.pdf
- **FSRS was developed and benchmarked on flashcard recall. Applying it to one card per concept with varied procedural items goes beyond what it was validated on; 02 itself notes the benchmark measures recall prediction, not skill transfer. In hierarchical subjects, practising an advanced topic implicitly reviews the simpler topics inside it. Math Academy's 'fractional implicit repetition' schedules for this, but it has no formal evaluation.** (practice (industry convention)). Sources: Skycak 2023, 'Optimized, Individualized Spaced Repetition in Hierarchical Knowledge Structures', https://justinmath.com/individualized-spaced-repetition-in-hierarchical-knowledge-structures/; knowledge/02 §4.2 and §6 caveats (benchmark measures recall prediction, not skill transfer)
- **Interleaving has a moderate overall effect (Hedges' g = 0.42, 59 studies). - **What SQL hiring assessments look like, from practitioner sources. Online assessments are about 1 hour for 2-3 questions. Live coding means saying your approach out loud. Take-homes use messy data. Topics include data modelling. The recommended approach is to clarify the question, consider edge cases (NULLs, ties), test step by step, then write clean code. Live-coding tools such as CoderPad offer MySQL or PostgreSQL. NL analyst funnels typically start with a timed online test and continue with business cases (01 §1.2).** (practice (industry convention)). A 42-person study found logic diagrams helped people interpret queries faster and with fewer errors. Because SQL is declarative, novices cannot trace it the way they trace imperative code, which supports a black-box view of the DBMS.** (weak (single study / expert opinion)). Sources: Akhuseyinoglu et al. 2022, ITiCSE, doi:10.1145/3502718.3524813 (DBQA step-through); Leventidis, Zhang, Dunne, Gatterbauer, Jagadish, Riedewald 2020, SIGMOD, 'QueryVis: Logic-based diagrams help users understand complicated SQL queries faster', https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.11375; Miedema, Aivaloglou, Fletcher 2021, ICER (discussion of tracing and visualisation); Taipalus & Seppänen 2020, TOCE, item PV5
- **Practitioner advice on portfolios: hiring teams discount tutorial and guided projects, and want the candidate's own question, real messy data, and an end-to-end story from problem to method to outcome.** (practice (industry convention)). Sources: Dataquest 2025, 'Building and presenting your data portfolio', https://dataquest.io/blog/building-and-presenting-your-data-portfolio
- **Solving a problem before instruction (productive failure) helps conceptual understanding when designed faithfully (meta-analysis of 53 studies and 166 comparisons; exact effect sizes not retrieved here). Asking questions before study (prequestions) has a moderate effect on the questioned content (g = 0.54) but almost none on content that was not asked about (g = 0.04). That meta-analysis covers prose material, not computation.** (moderate (several studies)). ### Learning science behind the aydinlearns v1 mechanics (spacing and FSRS on concept cards, interleaving, mastery thresholds, learner control a

- **Overall verdict: the spec's core mechanics agree with the evidence and are mostly well built. These are: spacing across days, interleaving confusable concepts after blocked first exposure, instruction-first lessons with backward-faded worked examples, elaborated task-focused feedback (row diff plus named misconception), mastery that needs varied items on more than one day, and no streaks. The weak points are not the big choices. They are about how evidence is counted: blocked and repeated items count as mastery evidence, interleaving is weakened by visible labels, one show-answer rule contradicts another, item pools are thin, 'I was right' counts at once, and there is no credit for prerequisites.** (strong (meta-analysis / replicated)). Sources: See the individual findings below; each carries its own sources
- **Spacing practice across days improves long-term retention of maths procedures, while extra practice in the same session adds nothing. In Rohrer & Taylor (2006, 216 students), spreading 10 problems over two sessions a week apart gave no benefit at 1 week but a very large one at 4 weeks. Doing 9 instead of 3 problems in one session (overlearning) made no difference at 1 or 4 weeks. Combining spacing with retrieval beats either alone (Latimier, Peyre & Ramus 2021, 29 studies), and expanding schedules showed little advantage over uniform ones.** (moderate (several studies)). Sources: Rohrer, D. & Taylor, K. (2006). The effects of overlearning and distributed practise on the retention of mathematics knowledge. Applied Cognitive Psychology. https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1770/; Latimier, A., Peyre, H. & Ramus, F. (2021). A meta-analytic review of the benefit of spacing out retrieval practice episodes on retention. Educational Psychology Review 33, 959-987
- **The spacing benefit shrinks as task complexity rises. Donovan & Radosevich (1999; 63 studies, 112 effects) found d = 0.46 overall, but d = 0.97 for simple motor tasks against 0.07-0.11 for high-complexity tasks. Overall complexity correlated r = -.25 with effect size, low-rigour studies inflated effects, and almost all 'retention' tests were at 24 h. Cognitive, accuracy-based skills still decay over months without use. Arthur et al. (1998) found they lose more than physical or speed-based tasks. Tatel & Ackerman (2025, 457 studies) found about half the gains lost after about 6.5 months on accuracy measures.** (strong (meta-analysis / replicated)). Sources: Donovan, J. J. & Radosevich, D. J. (1999). A meta-analytic review of the distribution of practice effect: Now you see it, now you don't. Journal of Applied Psychology 84(5), 795-805. https://www.gwern.net/docs/spaced-repetition/1999-donovan.pdf; Arthur, W. Jr., Bennett, W. Jr., Stanush, P. L. & McNelly, T. L. (1998). Factors that influence skill decay and retention: A quantitative review and analysis. Human Performance 11(1), 57-101; Tatel, C. E. & Ackerman, P. L. (2025). Procedural skill retention and decay: A meta-analytic review. Psychological Bulletin 151(6), 696-736. doi:10.1037/bul0000481
- **Memory models such as FSRS and SM-2 are fitted to repeated reviews of the same fixed prompt. I found no study that validates them for a concept card that shows a different problem at each review. Learner-modelling research handles this case differently. Knowledge-tracing models estimate skill across varied items (Pelánek 2017). DAS3H (Choffin et al. 2019, EDM best paper) predicted better by modelling item difficulty, multiple skill tags and forgetting together than models that handle only some of them. Lindsey et al. (2014) found that model-based personalised review at the knowledge-component level raised retention by 16.5% over massed study and 10% over generic spacing, but the material was vocabulary. Practitioner notes say varied prompts reduce answering by surface pattern (Matuschak).** (weak (single study / expert opinion)). Sources: Pelánek, R. (2017). Bayesian knowledge tracing, logistic models, and beyond: an overview of learner modeling techniques. User Modeling and User-Adapted Interaction 27, 313-350. doi:10.1007/s11257-017-9193-2; Choffin, B., Popineau, F., Bourda, Y. & Vie, J.-J. (2019). DAS3H: Modeling student learning and forgetting for optimally scheduling distributed practice of skills. EDM 2019. https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.06873; Lindsey, R. V., Shroyer, J. D., Pashler, H. & Mozer, M. C. (2014). Improving students' long-term knowledge retention through personalized review. Psychological Science. https://home.cs.colorado.edu/~mozer/Research/Selected%20Publications/reprints/LindseyShroyerPashlerMozer2014.pdf; Matuschak, A. Would spaced repetition memory systems perform better with varied question texts? https://notes.andymatuschak.org/z2HrihbnTYxjcu382ihWE9P; Anki Manual, Deck Options: FSRS. https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html
- **In skills that build on each other, practising an advanced item also exercises its prerequisites. Math Academy's 'Fractional Implicit Repetition' gives prerequisite topics partial review credit from advanced work, and picks reviews that clear other due reviews at the same time. This is a practitioner write-up, not peer-reviewed. DAS3H's results support modelling every skill an item touches.** (practice (industry convention)). Sources: Skycak, J. Optimized, individualized spaced repetition in hierarchical knowledge structures. https://justinmath.com/individualized-spaced-repetition-in-hierarchical-knowledge-structures/; Choffin et al. (2019), DAS3H, https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.06873
- **Performance during blocked practice overstates learning. In interleaving studies, blocked practice gives higher practice scores but lower delayed-test scores (Rohrer & Taylor 2007; Rohrer et al. 2020). Soderstrom & Bjork (2015) review many cases where training performance and long-term learning come apart or move in opposite directions. Learners also rate massed study as more effective even after doing worse with it (Kornell & Bjork 2008).** (strong (meta-analysis / replicated)). Sources: Rohrer, D. & Taylor, K. (2007). The shuffling of mathematics problems improves learning. Instructional Science 35(6), 481-498. doi:10.1007/s11251-007-9015-8; Soderstrom, N. C. & Bjork, R. A. (2015). - **Interleaving helps when problem types are easy to confuse. Brunmair & Richter (2019; 59 studies, 238 effects) found g = 0.42 overall, 0.34 for maths and 0.67 for paintings, with blocking better for word lists (g = -0.39). A classroom maths RCT found d = 0.83 on a delayed test (Rohrer, Dedrick, Hartwig & Cheung 2020, 787 students). In undergraduate physics, surprise tests showed d = 0.40 and 0.91, though the gain did not appear on later midterms (Samani & Pan 2021). Evidence for blocking first and interleaving afterwards is thin: one undergraduate-journal study, plus similarity-based accounts.** (strong (meta-analysis / replicated)). - **Interleaving works mainly because the learner has to work out from the problem itself which strategy it needs (Rohrer 2012). If the problem announces its type, that step disappears.** (moderate (several studies)). Sources: Rohrer, D. (2012). Interleaving helps students distinguish among similar concepts. Educational Psychology Review 24, 355-367. https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1755
- **Learners rate interleaved or spaced practice as harder, and believe they learned less from it, even when they learned more (Kornell & Bjork 2008; Samani & Pan 2021 call this a 'metacognitive illusion'). People often misjudge and mismanage their own study because they read fluency and current performance as learning (Bjork, Dunlosky & Kornell 2013).** (strong (meta-analysis / replicated)). Sources: Bjork, R. A., Dunlosky, J. & Kornell, N. (2013). Self-regulated learning: beliefs, techniques, and illusions. - **Mastery learning works: Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns (1990) found an average effect size of 0.52 across 103 studies. But where the threshold is set matters. Pelánek & Řihák (2017) found that the data used and the threshold matter more than which learner model is chosen, and recommend an exponential moving average. Kelly et al. (2015, EDM poster, ASSISTments) found that 3 correct in a row predicted the next item about 80% of the time. On a transfer question, though, 3 in a row gave many false positives (16 of the 33 students who reached it failed the transfer item), and 5 in a row was more accurate (small sample).** (moderate (several studies)). Sources: Kulik, C.-L. C., Kulik, J. A. & Bangert-Drowns, R. L. (1990). Effectiveness of mastery learning programs: A meta-analysis. Review of Educational Research 60(2); Pelánek, R. & Řihák, J. - **In tutors, some learners fail again and again to master a skill ('wheel-spinning', Beck & Gong 2013). More practice of the same kind rarely fixes it; these long failure runs need a different intervention.** (moderate (several studies)). Sources: Beck, J. E. & Gong, Y. (2013). Wheel-spinning: Students who fail to master a skill. AIED 2013, LNCS 7926, 431-440
- **On average, giving learners control has close to zero effect on learning. Karich, Burns & Maki (2014; 18 studies, 29 effects) found g = 0.05. Later reviews summarise Kraiger & Jerden (2007) as finding mild benefits; I did not retrieve that primary. For adult cognitive-skill training, Carolan et al. (2014) found learner control less effective than exploratory learning overall, with its benefits mainly on very near transfer. Novices lack the self-assessment and task-selection skills to steer well (Kirschner & van Merriënboer 2013). In Atkinson (1972), learner-chosen sequencing beat random order by 53% on a delayed test, and a model-based schedule beat it by 108%.** (strong (meta-analysis / replicated)). Sources: Karich, A. C., Burns, M. K. & Maki, K. E. (2014). Updated meta-analysis of learner control within educational technology. Review of Educational Research 84(3), 392-410; Carolan, T. F., Hutchins, S. D., Wickens, C. D. & Cumming, J. M. (2014). Costs and benefits of more learner freedom: Meta-analyses of exploratory and learner control training methods. Human Factors. doi:10.1177/0018720813517710; Kirschner, P. A. & van Merriënboer, J. J. G. (2013). Do learners really know best? Urban legends in education. Educational Psychologist 48(3); Atkinson, R. C. (1972). Optimizing the learning of a second-language vocabulary. Journal of Experimental Psychology 96, 124-129. doi:10.1037/h0033475; Kraiger, K. & Jerden, E. (2007), cited via secondary sources only, e.g. https://arxiv.org/html/2505.22526v1
- **On-demand help helps, but only so much (Aleven et al. 2016). Misusing help can make learning shallow (the assistance dilemma, Koedinger & Aleven 2007). In fine-grained tutor data, asking for help on hard steps went with productive learning and overusing help with poorer learning. On steps where the learner had low prior knowledge, trying and failing first went with better learning than asking at once (Roll et al. 2014; 38 students, correlational). Copying physics homework answers correlated with about a 2 SD relative drop on similar exam problems (Palazzo et al. 2010). Metacognitive feedback on help-seeking changed how help was used, but not domain learning (Roll et al. 2011; Aleven et al. 2016). Hints that let learners keep retrieving raised voluntary self-testing without lowering learning (Vaughn & Kornell 2019, vocabulary).** (moderate (several studies)).
